\section{Raw Charge Asymmetry}
\label{sec:cp}

The selected candidates can be separated into charge-conjugate samples using the
total charge of the three daughter tracks,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mathrm{tot}} = Q(H_1) + Q(H_2) + Q(H_3).
\end{equation}
Candidates with $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}=+1$ are classified as \Bplus{} candidates,
while candidates with $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}=-1$ are classified as \Bminus{}
candidates. This assignment uses only reconstructed track-charge information
and is therefore a natural first step toward a charge-asymmetry study in a
three-charged-hadron final state. The selected sample contains $884$ \Bplus{}
and $761$ \Bminus{} candidates.

\subsection{Charge-separated invariant-mass fits}

Rather than counting candidates in a fixed signal window, the asymmetry is
extracted by fitting the \Bplus{} and \Bminus{} invariant-mass spectra
separately, with the nominal model of Sec.~\ref{sec:mass_fit}: a single Gaussian
signal and a finite-range exponential background over
$5200 < m(hhh) < 5600~\mevcc$. Fitting rather than counting matters here,
because a fixed window mixes the background into the yield, and the background
fraction need not be the same for the two charges.

The fitted spectra are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:charge_fit_plus} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:charge_fit_minus}. Both fits converge to a valid minimum with a
positive-definite covariance matrix. Goodness of fit is judged with the
likelihood-ratio $\chi^2$ of Baker and Cousins~\cite{bakercousins}, which uses every bin including
the empty ones. With many bins holding only a few candidates, its asymptotic
$\chi^2$ distribution does not hold --- the asymptotic p-value for \Bplus{} is
$0.18$ --- so the p-value is taken from the $2000$ pseudo-experiments of the
toy study below, each refitted: $p = 0.035 \pm 0.004$ for \Bplus{} and
$p = 0.43 \pm 0.01$ for \Bminus{}. The \Bplus{} description is therefore
marginal, at about two standard deviations. The fitted signal yields
are
\begin{align}
  N(\Bplus) &= 689.1 \pm 28.6, \\
  N(\Bminus) &= 642.1 \pm 26.2,
\end{align}
where the uncertainties are the statistical yield uncertainties returned by
the binned likelihood fits.

\subsection{Raw asymmetry extraction}

The fitted signal yields are used to construct the raw asymmetry
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} =
  \frac{N(\Bminus)-N(\Bplus)}
       {N(\Bminus)+N(\Bplus)} .
  \label{eq:raw_asymmetry_fit}
\end{equation}
Propagating the independent fitted-yield uncertainties gives
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} = -0.0353 \pm 0.0290~\mathrm{(stat.)}.
\end{equation}
After including the systematic variations described below, the result is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} =
  -0.0353 \pm 0.0290~\mathrm{(stat.)}
  \pm 0.0437~\mathrm{(syst.)},
  \label{eq:raw_asymmetry_result}
\end{equation}
that is, compatible with zero at the level of $0.7$ standard deviations of the
total uncertainty.

This is a raw asymmetry, not a physical CP-asymmetry measurement. It still
contains detector-induced charge asymmetries, possible \Bplus{}/\Bminus{}
production asymmetries, trigger and reconstruction effects, residual
background-model dependence, and selection-induced charge biases, and it uses a
single magnet polarity. A complete measurement would require detector
calibration, magnet-polarity combination, background control, efficiency
corrections, a control channel to cancel production and detection asymmetries,
and a more complete systematic uncertainty programme. The value in
Eq.~\ref{eq:raw_asymmetry_result} should therefore be interpreted as a
diagnostic of the analysis workflow rather than as evidence for CP violation.

For orientation only, the published LHCb measurement of the inclusive CP
asymmetry in $B^\pm \to K^\pm K^+ K^-$, using the full 2011 dataset with all
corrections applied, is $A_{CP} = -0.043 \pm 0.009~\mathrm{(stat.)} \pm
0.003~\mathrm{(syst.)} \pm 0.007~(J/\psi K^\pm)$~\cite{lhcb3body}. The raw value
obtained here is of the same sign and magnitude, with an uncertainty about four
times larger. That numerical proximity is encouraging but must not be read as a
validation: none of the corrections that turn a raw asymmetry into a CP
asymmetry have been applied, and the uncorrected detector and production effects
are not known to be small compared with the quoted uncertainty.

\subsection{Toy-MC validation}

The statistical machinery was validated with pseudo-experiments generated from
the nominal charge-separated fitted model. In each toy experiment, \Bplus{} and
\Bminus{} mass spectra were generated with the injected fitted yields and then
refitted with the same model used in the data fit. The toy study checks whether
the fitting procedure and the uncertainty propagation recover the injected raw
asymmetry when the fit model is correct.

A study of $2000$ toys with a fixed random seed produced no fit failures and no
toy rejected from the pull study. The mean raw-asymmetry pull is $-0.003$ and
the pull width is $0.9999$, both consistent with a standard normal distribution.
The fitted bias is below $10^{-4}$, four orders of magnitude smaller than the
statistical uncertainty. The mean fitted uncertainty, $0.02889$, matches the
observed spread of the fitted asymmetries, $0.02888$, so the uncertainty
returned by the fit is correctly calibrated. This validates the statistical
extraction under a correct model; it is not a detector-level systematic study,
and it cannot detect a bias caused by the model itself being wrong.

\subsection{Systematic uncertainties}

Two analysis choices are varied: the mass-fit model and the invariant-mass fit
range. A configuration is accepted only if the \Bplus{} and \Bminus{} fits
converge to a valid minimum, the fitted yields and yield uncertainties are
positive, and the $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}$ values remain below $2.5$ for both
charge-separated spectra. These criteria were fixed before the variations were
run. Because the double-Gaussian model has a nearly degenerate direction, each
fit is started from several points and the lowest valid minimum is kept; with a
single starting point the double-Gaussian fits failed to converge for one charge
and were rejected for that reason alone.

\paragraph{One source at a time.} The fit-model study compares the nominal
single Gaussian plus exponential model with a single Gaussian plus finite-range
linear background and with two double-Gaussian alternatives. All three are
accepted. The double-Gaussian variations shift the raw asymmetry by $-0.027$
and $-0.031$: their wide component, $\sigma \approx 53~\mevcc$, takes over the
combinatorial background, whose fitted yield falls to values compatible with
zero. The largest shift, $0.031$, is the fit-model diagnostic
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fit_model_systematic}).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fit_model_variations.png}
  \caption{Raw asymmetry obtained with different charge-separated mass-fit models on the nominal fit range.}
  \label{fig:fit_model_systematic}
\end{figure}

The fit-range study repeats the nominal fits for narrower and wider intervals.
A change of range does not only change the analysis choice: it adds or removes
candidates, and the two results would differ even with no systematic effect.
The expected size of that statistical difference is
$\sigma_{\mathrm{B}} = \sqrt{|\sigma_{\mathrm{var}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{nom}}^2|}$
(Barlow~\cite{barlow}), and only the part of a shift $\Delta$ beyond it,
$\sqrt{\max(\Delta^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{B}}^2, 0)}$, is evidence of a systematic
effect. Raising the lower edge from $5200$ to $5230~\mevcc$ shifts the
asymmetry by $\Delta = 0.031$ with $\sigma_{\mathrm{B}} = 0.015$; a toy study that
fits the same pseudo-data on both ranges gives a spread of $0.0144 \pm 0.0004$
and no mean shift, confirming the formula. The shift is $2.1\,\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}$,
a genuine effect of the data, and its Barlow-corrected size, $0.027$, is the
fit-range diagnostic (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit_range_systematic}).

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{fit_range_variations.png}
  \caption{Raw asymmetry obtained for alternative invariant-mass fit ranges using the nominal signal and background model.}
  \label{fig:fit_range_systematic}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Both sources together.} The two diagnostics cannot be added in
quadrature, because they are not independent. Repeating every model on every
range (Fig.~\ref{fig:joint_variations}) shows that the sensitivity to the lower
edge belongs to the nominal model alone: with any other model, raising the edge
to $5230~\mevcc$ moves the asymmetry by at most $0.006$. The nominal exponential
is steep enough to absorb a shoulder on the low-mass flank of the \Bplus{} peak
that the \Bminus{} spectrum does not show; narrowing the range removes that
shoulder from the fit, while the other models attribute it to the signal or to a
flatter background from the start. Both diagnostics are symptoms of the same
unmodelled structure.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{joint_variations.png}
  \caption{Shift of the raw asymmetry from the nominal for every combination of fit model and fit range. All 20 configurations pass the acceptance criteria.}
  \label{fig:joint_variations}
\end{figure}

The systematic uncertainty is therefore taken as the largest shift from the
nominal over all $20$ accepted configurations, $0.0437$, reached by the
double-Gaussian model on $5200$--$5550~\mevcc$. This measures the joint variation
directly instead of assuming a correlation. The range shifts inside it are not
Barlow-corrected, so it is mildly conservative. The nominal also sits at the
edge of the grid: $16$ of the $19$ other configurations give a more negative
asymmetry.

  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{systematic_breakdown.png}

The systematic now exceeds the statistical uncertainty, and it is dominated by a
single feature of the data: the low-mass structure in the \Bplus{} spectrum,
which no tested model describes. Modelling it explicitly --- a
partially-reconstructed or misidentified component fitted over a wider range,
rather than excluded --- is the first step a real measurement would need. Beyond
that, signal-shape variations constrained by simulation, magnet-polarity
comparisons, a control channel and detector-efficiency corrections would all be
required before interpreting the raw asymmetry as a corrected CP observable.
