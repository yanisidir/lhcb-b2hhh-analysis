\section{Raw Charge Asymmetry}
\label{sec:cp}

The selected candidates are separated into charge-conjugate samples using the
total charge of the three daughter tracks,
\begin{equation}
  Q_{\mathrm{tot}} = Q(H_1) + Q(H_2) + Q(H_3).
\end{equation}
Candidates with $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}=+1$ are classified as \Bplus{} candidates and
those with $Q_{\mathrm{tot}}=-1$ as \Bminus{} candidates. The selected samples
contain $884$ \Bplus{} and $761$ \Bminus{} candidates in MagnetDown, and $658$ and
$636$ in MagnetUp.

\subsection{Simultaneous charge fits}

The asymmetry is extracted from fitted yields rather than from counts in a fixed
window, which would mix the background into the yield. For each magnet polarity,
the \Bplus{} and \Bminus{} spectra over $5200 < m(hhh) < 5600~\mevcc$ are fitted
\emph{simultaneously} with the nominal model of Sec.~\ref{sec:mass_fit}: the peak
position, the resolution and the background slope are shared between the two
charges, and each charge has its own signal and background yields. The signal
yields are parameterised by their sum $N$ and the raw asymmetry
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} =
  \frac{N(\Bminus)-N(\Bplus)}
       {N(\Bminus)+N(\Bplus)} ,
  \label{eq:raw_asymmetry_fit}
\end{equation}
which is therefore a parameter of the fit, with an uncertainty that includes its
correlation with $N$.

Sharing the shapes is a physics statement: neither the mass resolution nor the
shape of a combinatorial background has a reason to differ between the two
charges. It also matters in practice. When the two MagnetDown spectra are fitted
separately, the \Bplus{} background takes a slope of $-9.5 \pm 1.4$ per
$\mathrm{GeV}/c^2$ against $-6.0 \pm 2.1$ for \Bminus{}, and $83$ background
candidates against $31$; a fluctuation of the \Bplus{} spectrum
(Sec.~\ref{sec:shoulder}) is then absorbed by its background, which moves its
signal yield and makes the asymmetry depend on where the fit range starts. With
shared shapes the background can only change its yield, not its shape, to follow
it.

All four fits converge to a valid minimum with a positive-definite covariance
matrix (Figs.~\ref{fig:charge_fits_down} and~\ref{fig:charge_fits_up}). They give
\begin{align}
  \text{MagnetDown:}\quad N(\Bplus) &= 686 \pm 28, \quad N(\Bminus) = 641 \pm 26,
  \quad A_{\mathrm{raw}} = -0.0337 \pm 0.0286, \\
  \text{MagnetUp:}\quad N(\Bplus) &= 548 \pm 24, \quad N(\Bminus) = 515 \pm 24,
  \quad A_{\mathrm{raw}} = -0.0305 \pm 0.0315,
\end{align}
with a common peak position of $5284.7 \pm 0.6$ and $5284.8 \pm 0.6~\mevcc$ and a
resolution of $19.4 \pm 0.5~\mevcc$ in both polarities.

Goodness of fit is judged with the likelihood-ratio $\chi^2$ of Baker and
Cousins~\cite{bakercousins}, which uses every bin including the empty ones. With
many bins holding only a few candidates, its asymptotic $\chi^2$ distribution
does not hold, so the p-value is taken from the pseudo-experiments of the toy
study below, each refitted: $p = 0.025 \pm 0.003$ for MagnetDown and
$p = 0.30 \pm 0.01$ for MagnetUp. The MagnetDown description is marginal, for the
reason given in Sec.~\ref{sec:shoulder}.

\subsection{Combination of the magnet polarities}

Reversing the magnet swaps the side of the detector towards which each charge is
bent. A left--right detection asymmetry therefore enters the two polarities with
opposite signs, and the \emph{equal-weight} average
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(A_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{Down}} + A_{\mathrm{raw}}^{\mathrm{Up}}\right),
  \qquad
  \sigma = \tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\sigma_{\mathrm{Down}}^2 + \sigma_{\mathrm{Up}}^2},
\end{equation}
cancels it to first order. A precision-weighted average would not, because the
two samples differ in size. The two polarities agree well, and the average is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} = -0.0321 \pm 0.0213~\mathrm{(stat.)}.
\end{equation}
After including the systematic variations described below, the result is
\begin{equation}
  A_{\mathrm{raw}} =
  -0.0321 \pm 0.0213~\mathrm{(stat.)}
  \pm 0.0163~\mathrm{(syst.)},
  \label{eq:raw_asymmetry_result}
\end{equation}
compatible with zero at the level of $1.2$ standard deviations of the total
uncertainty.

This is a raw asymmetry, not a physical CP-asymmetry measurement. The polarity
average removes the polarity-odd part of the detection asymmetry, but not the
kaon detection asymmetry that survives it, the \Bplus{}/\Bminus{} production
asymmetry, trigger and reconstruction effects, or selection-induced charge
biases. A complete measurement would require detector calibration, background
control, efficiency corrections, a control channel to cancel production and
detection asymmetries, and a more complete systematic programme. The value in
Eq.~\ref{eq:raw_asymmetry_result} should therefore be interpreted as a diagnostic
of the analysis workflow rather than as evidence for CP violation.

For orientation only, the published LHCb measurement of the inclusive CP
asymmetry in $B^\pm \to K^\pm K^+ K^-$, using the full 2011 dataset with all
corrections applied, is $A_{CP} = -0.043 \pm 0.009~\mathrm{(stat.)} \pm
0.003~\mathrm{(syst.)} \pm 0.007~(J/\psi K^\pm)$~\cite{lhcb3body}. The raw value
obtained here is of the same sign and magnitude, with a total uncertainty a little
over twice as large. That numerical proximity is encouraging but must not be read
as a validation: none of the corrections that turn a raw asymmetry into a CP
asymmetry have been applied, and the uncorrected detector and production effects
are not known to be small compared with the quoted uncertainty.

\subsection{The low-mass shoulder of the MagnetDown \Bplus{} spectrum}
\label{sec:shoulder}

The MagnetDown \Bplus{} spectrum shows an excess on the low-mass flank of the
peak (Fig.~\ref{fig:low_mass_flank}): $105$ candidates between $5220$ and
$5260~\mevcc$, where the shared-shape fit expects $91$, against $56$ for $73$
expected in \Bminus{}. Only $5$ of the $61$ \Bplus{} candidates between $5200$ and
$5250~\mevcc$ have a $K^+K^-$ pair compatible with a $\bar D^0$, and the excess
lies above the endpoint of the partially reconstructed structure of
Sec.~\ref{sec:mass_fit}.

Whether the two charges share a shape is tested with the likelihood ratio of the
simultaneous fit against separate fits, which have three more parameters (peak
position, resolution and background slope for each charge). The statistic is
calibrated with $1000$ pseudo-experiments generated from the shared-shape fit and
refitted both ways. On MagnetDown it gives $p = 0.043 \pm 0.006$. That overstates
the evidence, since the flank was singled out after looking at this spectrum. The
independent MagnetUp sample settles the question: it shows no excess ($53$
candidates for $62$ expected in \Bplus{}, $66$ for $58$ in \Bminus{}) and gives
$p = 0.25 \pm 0.01$. A genuine physics effect would appear in MagnetUp \Bplus{}
as well, and a polarity-dependent detector effect would move to MagnetUp
\Bminus{}; neither is seen at a significant level. The shoulder is treated as a
fluctuation of about two standard deviations. It is the reason the MagnetDown fit
is marginal, and, through the separate fits, it was the origin of the fit-range
dependence of the earlier version of this analysis.

One weaker pattern is recorded without being claimed. In separate fits the
\Bplus{} and \Bminus{} peak positions differ by $-2.5 \pm 1.2~\mevcc$ in
MagnetDown and by $+1.8 \pm 1.3~\mevcc$ in MagnetUp. A sign flip with the
polarity is what an uncalibrated charge-dependent momentum scale would produce,
but each value is only about two standard deviations from zero; a momentum-scale
calibration would be the first thing to check in a real measurement.

\subsection{Toy-MC validation}

The statistical machinery is validated with $2000$ pseudo-experiments. In each,
the \Bplus{} and \Bminus{} spectra of both polarities are generated from that
polarity's fitted model, refitted simultaneously, and averaged over the
polarities exactly as the data are. No fit fails. The raw-asymmetry pull has mean
$-0.03$ and width $0.98$; the bias of the average is $-0.0005$, $2.6\%$ of its
statistical uncertainty. The mean fitted uncertainty, $0.0212$, slightly exceeds
the observed spread of the fitted asymmetries, $0.0207$, so the quoted
uncertainty is calibrated and, if anything, conservative. This validates the
statistical extraction under a correct model; it is not a detector-level
systematic study, and it cannot detect a bias caused by the model itself being
wrong.

\subsection{Systematic uncertainties}

Two analysis choices are varied: the mass-fit model and the invariant-mass fit
range. Every configuration is evaluated exactly as the nominal: both polarities
are fitted simultaneously in charge and averaged. A configuration is accepted only
if every fit converges to a valid minimum, the fitted yields and their
uncertainties are positive, and the $\chi^2/\mathrm{ndof}$ of each charge
spectrum remains below $2.5$. These criteria were fixed before the variations were
run. Because the double-Gaussian model has a nearly degenerate direction, each fit
is started from several points and the lowest valid minimum is kept.

The fit-model and fit-range variations are not assumed to be independent. Every
model is refitted on every range (Fig.~\ref{fig:joint_variations}), and the
systematic uncertainty is the largest shift from the nominal over all accepted
configurations. All $20$ configurations pass the acceptance criteria. The largest
shift, $0.0163$, comes from the double Gaussian with a linear background on
$5200$--$5700~\mevcc$. The range shifts inside the envelope are not
Barlow-corrected, so it is mildly conservative.

  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{joint_variations.png}
  \caption{Shift of the polarity-averaged raw asymmetry from the nominal for every combination of fit model and fit range. All 20 configurations pass the acceptance criteria.}
  \label{fig:joint_variations}

The one-at-a-time variations are kept as diagnostics. The fit-model study
compares the nominal with a single Gaussian plus finite-range linear background
and with two double-Gaussian alternatives; the largest shift is $0.014$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fit_model_systematic}). The fit-range study repeats the nominal fit
on narrower and wider intervals (Fig.~\ref{fig:fit_range_systematic}). A change
of range adds or removes candidates, so the two results differ even with no
systematic effect; the expected size of that difference is
$\sigma_{\mathrm{B}} = \sqrt{|\sigma_{\mathrm{var}}^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{nom}}^2|}$
(Barlow~\cite{barlow}), and only the part of a shift $\Delta$ beyond it,
$\sqrt{\max(\Delta^2 - \sigma_{\mathrm{B}}^2, 0)}$, is evidence of a systematic
effect. With shared shapes every range shift is within $1.5\,\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}$;
the largest Barlow-corrected shift is $0.0075$. In the earlier version of this
analysis, with separate charge fits on MagnetDown alone, raising the lower edge to
$5230~\mevcc$ moved the asymmetry by $2.1\,\sigma_{\mathrm{B}}$; that sensitivity
came from the \Bplus{} background shape following the shoulder, and it is gone.

The envelope is dominated by MagnetDown. Across the $20$ configurations its
asymmetry ranges from $-0.015$ to $-0.068$, while MagnetUp stays between $-0.023$
and $-0.032$: the model dependence comes from how each model treats the MagnetDown
\Bplus{} shoulder. The nominal also sits towards one edge of the grid, with $14$
of the $19$ other configurations giving a more negative asymmetry.

The statistical uncertainty now dominates. These studies probe only a limited set
of analysis choices: signal-shape variations constrained by simulation, a
momentum-scale calibration, a control channel and detector-efficiency corrections
would all be required before interpreting the raw asymmetry as a corrected CP
observable.
